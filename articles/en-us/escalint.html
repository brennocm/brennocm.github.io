escalint: AWS IAM privilege escalation paths, made visual

Oct 23, 2026 by Brenno M.

An IAM principal rarely has AdministratorAccess directly. What it usually has is iam:PassRole plus lambda:CreateFunction, or iam:CreatePolicyVersion on a policy attached to itself, or permission to assume a role whose trust policy it can rewrite. Read on its own, none of these policies looks dangerous. The problem shows up when permissions from different principals and accounts are put together.

I wrote escalint, an open source tool in Go, to find those combinations. It reads an IAM snapshot of one or more AWS accounts, evaluates the policies the way AWS does, builds a graph of who can act as whom and draws every path that ends in administrator, with an explanation for each step and the smallest set of changes it finds to cut those paths.

There is a live demo with synthetic data that runs entirely in the browser.

Existing tools

For attackers

Attackers have modern, maintained tools for AWS. Rhino Security Labs' Pacu is an exploitation framework with modules that enumerate permissions and carry out the escalation. Bishop Fox's CloudFox maps an account's attack surface from compromised credentials. Datadog's Stratus Red Team emulates cloud attack techniques mapped to MITRE ATT&CK. They gain support for new services and newly published techniques shortly after those appear.

For defenders

For defenders who want to know effective access in IAM, the options are weaker:

  1. Prowler is the reference open source CSPM and covers a lot: hundreds of configuration checks across several providers (public S3, open security groups, encryption, logging). Its escalation checks look at one policy at a time, searching for dangerous combinations of actions, and do not answer a question like "can this principal, by assuming that role, reach admin in two steps?".
  2. NCC Group's PMapper was the first to treat IAM as a graph. It has gone years without a meaningful update, and its catalog of techniques and services has stood still.
  3. Duo Labs' CloudMapper stopped in 2022. Salesforce's Cloudsplaining produces good reports on overly permissive policies, but it analyzes each policy in isolation and its future is uncertain.

In December 2025, Datadog published pathfinding.cloud, an open catalog of AWS privilege escalation paths. At launch, 27 of the 65 documented paths (42%) were not detected by any of the open source tools evaluated. The catalog now has 92 paths. For many of them, the attacker has a framework that runs the technique and the defender has nothing that flags it beforehand.


Why a graph

John Lambert of Microsoft has a well-known line: "Defenders think in lists. Attackers think in graphs. As long as this is true, attackers win." Active Directory went through this. BloodHound started as an offensive tool and was adopted by defense teams, because a graph of attack paths shows relationships that a list of findings hides.

IAM on AWS has the same shape. A list says "role X has iam:PassRole on *", medium severity, somewhere among two hundred other items. The graph says "the CI user can launch an EC2 instance with role Y, which can attach a policy to itself and becomes account admin", with the whole sequence and the place where it can be cut.

The graph also changes how you fix things. When ten paths go through the same edge, that edge is a choke point, and removing it fixes all ten at once. In a list, they show up as ten unrelated items.

Policy evaluation

At the core is an evaluator that follows AWS's documented logic: explicit deny in any policy, SCPs and RCPs at each OU level, permissions boundaries, identity policies, trust policies and cross-account rules, including NotAction, NotResource, NotPrincipal and wildcards in actions and ARNs. Every Allow comes with a trace naming the statement that decided it. Conditions that cannot be evaluated statically (MFA, source IP, time of day) stay in the graph: the step is marked conditional and drawn dashed.

The graph and the rules

Nodes are principals (users, roles, groups), plus one sink per account, Tier 0, which stands for effective admin. Edges come from 39 rules: 30 escalation paths (EL-PE), 6 trust mistakes (EL-TR, for example GitHub Actions OIDC with no condition on sub) and 3 input diagnostics (EL-PARSE). Every rule cites the AWS documentation, its pathfinding.cloud ID and the matching MITRE ATT&CK techniques. The current version covers 33 of the catalog's 92 paths.

Analyses

On top of the graph, escalint computes the k shortest paths from each principal to Tier 0, privilege tiers, blast radius, assume-role cycles, choke points and a greedy remediation plan, meaning an ordered sequence of changes that removes every path. For each step it also lists which events CloudTrail would record, or whether the step is a blind spot.


The interface

The UI is a strict TypeScript React app with its own WebGL renderer, embedded in the binary. It runs locally with escalint serve (only on 127.0.0.1, with a single-use token) or as a single offline HTML file produced by escalint report --html. There are nine views:

  1. Overview: tiers, critical paths, CloudTrail blind spots and what to fix first.
  2. Atlas: every principal in the account (or organization), grouped by account, with semantic zoom. Shift+click on two nodes shows the shortest path between them.
  3. Trace: one path step by step, with alternatives and choke points. Clicking an edge opens the policy explainer with the exact statement that allowed that step.
  4. Reach: what a principal can reach and who can reach it, in rings by hop count.
  5. Exposure, Findings, Fix plan, Detect and Diff: a principal × service matrix, a filterable table, a remediation plan with a live preview of the remaining paths, CloudTrail events per step and what changed between two snapshots.

Every graph has an equivalent table, navigation works from the keyboard and the palettes are colorblind-safe. The snapshot comes from an account that may contain hostile names and policies, so the UI uses a strict CSP and never uses innerHTML. To share a report, --redact replaces every identifier with a pseudonym.


Hands-on

Release binaries (Linux, macOS and Windows, amd64 and arm64) ship with the UI embedded, are signed with cosign and carry GitHub provenance attestations. There is also a distroless image: 

# pick your platform at https://github.com/brennocm/escalint/releases
curl -sSLO https://github.com/brennocm/escalint/releases/download/v0.1.0/escalint_0.1.0_linux_amd64.tar.gz
gh attestation verify escalint_0.1.0_linux_amd64.tar.gz --repo brennocm/escalint
tar xzf escalint_0.1.0_linux_amd64.tar.gz escalint

docker run --rm -v "$PWD:/work" ghcr.io/brennocm/escalint:v0.1.0 scan /work/snapshot.json

Basic use is three commands. collect only makes read calls (List* and Get*), and the repository publishes the minimal IAM policy it needs: 

escalint collect -o snapshot.json   # read-only snapshot with your AWS credentials
escalint serve snapshot.json        # opens the UI on 127.0.0.1 with a one-time token
escalint scan snapshot.json         # findings in the terminal; exit 1 on high or critical

If you have no credentials at hand, use the example accounts in the repository. This is the real output (trimmed) of scan on the vulnerable account:

Each finding points to the policy and snapshot line that caused it, the ATT&CK techniques and a concrete fix. Exit codes let escalint block a pipeline: 0 with no findings above --fail-on (default high), 1 with findings and 2 for a usage error.

In CI

The use I care about most is keeping a new path from showing up unnoticed. escalint diff compares two snapshots and fails only on added edges, paths and findings. The repository ships a GitHub Action for a daily job: it collects with a read-only role over OIDC, compares against the previous day's snapshot and uploads the result as SARIF to GitHub Code Scanning: 

escalint diff baseline.json snapshot.json --fail-on high
escalint scan snapshot.json --format sarif --output escalint.sarif

Where escalint fits

For overall account posture, Prowler is still the right tool; escalint does not look at public buckets or open security groups. For exploitation and offensive validation, use Pacu and CloudFox; escalint does not assume roles, create credentials or change anything in the account.

escalint takes PMapper's place: defensive, static analysis of effective IAM access, with an explanation for every step. Compared with it, escalint adds SCPs and RCPs, a current catalog mapped to pathfinding.cloud, CI-ready output and a graphical interface. The idea of a self-hosted graph where no data leaves the account came from marcelogsantos' aws-iam-graph; escalint was written from scratch.


Limitations

v1 does not model session policies and session tags, resource-based policies other than the trust policy (S3 bucket policies, KMS key policies, Lambda, Secrets Manager), root users or IAM Identity Center assignments. Request conditions are not known statically, so the affected steps stay conditional. Coverage is 33 of pathfinding.cloud's 92 paths; most of the missing ones pass a role to another compute service and follow the same pattern as the existing rules. The resource inventory is regional, the remediation plan is greedy (small, but not guaranteed to be minimal) and a snapshot is only valid for the moment it was collected: after changes, collect again.

The snapshot and the reports show who can become administrator in the account. Treat them as sensitive data and share them only with --redact.


Final thoughts

IAM privilege escalation usually comes from several reasonable permissions, spread across different principals and accounts, that nobody reviewed together. Attackers have tools that find and walk those combinations; defenders, most of the time, have a list of findings.

escalint evaluates the policies the way AWS does, draws the graph, explains each edge with the statement that allows it and suggests where to cut. That does not guarantee an account is secure, but it gives defenders the same graph an attacker would build.
