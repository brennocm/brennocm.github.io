CompTIA Security+ (SY0-701) Review

Sep 06, 2026 by Brenno M.

Security+, offered by CompTIA, is the most widely recognized entry-level certification in information security. It is vendor-neutral and covers a broad baseline: fundamental security concepts, threats and vulnerabilities, architecture, security operations, and governance, risk and compliance (GRC). It is also the baseline requirement for a large share of SOC, blue team, and junior security analyst roles worldwide.

On 09/06/2026, I took the exam and passed.

Topics Covered

The current exam version is SY0-701, split into five weighted domains. The certification covers the following topics:

  • General Security Concepts (12%)
    • Types and categories of security controls (technical, managerial, operational, physical / preventive, deterrent, detective, corrective, compensating, directive)
    • Fundamental concepts: CIA triad, non-repudiation, AAA, gap analysis, and Zero Trust
    • Physical security and change management processes
    • Cryptographic solutions: PKI, certificates, hashing, digital signatures, TPM, HSM, and obfuscation
  • Threats, Vulnerabilities, and Mitigations (22%)
    • Threat actors, motivations, and sophistication levels
    • Threat vectors and attack surfaces
    • Vulnerabilities in applications, operating systems, cloud, mobile, supply chain, and virtualization
    • Indicators of malicious activity: malware, network attacks, social engineering, and cryptographic attacks
    • Mitigation techniques and hardening
  • Security Architecture (18%)
    • Architecture models: cloud, IaC, serverless, microservices, SDN, on-premises, and IoT/SCADA/ICS
    • Infrastructure security: segmentation, firewalls, IDS/IPS, WAF, VPN, proxies, and SASE
    • Data protection: classification, encryption, DLP, tokenization, and masking
    • Resilience and recovery: high availability, backups, alternate sites, RTO/RPO, and continuity testing
  • Security Operations (28%)
    • Hardening endpoints, servers, mobile, and network devices
    • Hardware, software, and data asset management
    • Vulnerability management: scanning, CVSS, CVE, prioritization, and remediation
    • Alerting and monitoring: SIEM, SNMP, NetFlow, agents, and antivirus
    • Identity and access management: SSO, LDAP, SAML, OAuth, MFA, PAM, and authorization models
    • Automation and orchestration (SOAR) in secure operations
    • Incident response: preparation, detection, containment, eradication, recovery, and lessons learned
    • Data sources to support an investigation: logs, dashboards, packet captures, and forensic artifacts
  • Security Program Management and Oversight (20%)
    • Governance: policies, standards, procedures, roles, and decision-making structures
    • Risk management: identification, qualitative and quantitative analysis (SLE, ALE, ARO), treatment, and risk register
    • Third-party risk: due diligence, SLA, MOU, MSA, BPA, and vendor assessment
    • Compliance: privacy, consequences of non-compliance, monitoring, and attestation
    • Audits and assessments: internal, external, penetration testing, and rules of engagement
    • Security awareness: simulated phishing, anomalous behavior, and continuous training

About the exam

The exam consists of up to 90 questions in 90 minutes, mixing multiple-choice questions with PBQs (Performance-Based Questions), interactive simulations where you must configure firewall rules, correlate logs, classify attacks from evidence, or map controls to scenarios. The passing score is 750 on a 100-900 scale. There is no "with merit" distinction: the result is simply pass or fail, with the score shown on screen at the end.

The PBQs came up right at the start of my exam and were the main source of time pressure: each one took me between 5 and 10 minutes. I flagged all of them for review, answered the multiple-choice questions first, and only went back to the simulations with the time I had left. That is what kept me from losing easy questions for lack of time.

CompTIA informally recommends Network+ plus roughly two years of experience in systems administration or security. It is not mandatory, but the networking foundation makes a real difference: much of Security Architecture and Security Operations assumes you understand subnets, ports, protocols, and traffic flow.

The exam can be taken at an authorized Pearson VUE test center or remotely (online proctored). In the remote format, camera and microphone access are required, along with a physical environment check and a completely clear desk. The voucher costs around US$ 425 at list price, with lower prices through authorized resellers and bundles with official material. The certification is valid for 3 years and can be renewed two ways. The first is accumulating 50 CEUs over the cycle, subject to the annual maintenance fee. The second is passing a higher-level CompTIA certification within that same three-year cycle, such as CySA+, PenTest+, or SecurityX: renewal is then automatic, the Security+ maintenance fee is waived, and the expiration date realigns with that of the new certification.

One note on timing: SY0-701 is the current version, and CompTIA has already signaled SY0-801 for late 2026 / early 2027, with the English SY0-701 scheduled to retire in June 2027. If you are already studying for the 701, there is no reason to wait: the certification you earn is fully valid for the same 3 years regardless of exam version.

How the exam asks

The difficulty of Security+ is breadth, not technical depth. There are hundreds of acronyms, controls, frameworks, and protocols, tested through scenario-worded questions. The question is almost never "what is X?", but "given this situation, what is the BEST action/control/tool?".

The patterns that weighed the most on my exam, and that I recommend internalizing before sitting it:
  • Capitalized keywords (BEST, MOST, FIRST, MOST likely) completely change the answer. Often two or three options are technically correct and only one is best for the described context.
  • Think like CompTIA, not like the real world. The expected answer follows the process and best practices from the official material, not the shortcut that would work in your own environment.
  • Eliminate before choosing. Discarding two options was faster and more reliable, for me, than trying to justify the correct one outright.
  • Memorize the essentials: common ports and protocols, the order of incident response phases, the difference between control types and categories, and the risk formulas (SLE = AV × EF, ALE = SLE × ARO). These are guaranteed points.
  • Watch out for similar terms: authentication × authorization × accounting, vulnerability × threat × risk, IDS × IPS, RTO × RPO × MTTR × MTBF, SAML × OAuth × OpenID Connect.
  • Practice exams are mandatory. More than reviewing content, they train question reading and the ~1 minute per question pace. I only booked the exam after consistently reaching above 85% on them.

How RouteSec got me through

The RouteSec course, taught by Hugo B., is the material that sustained my preparation. I used other sources alongside it, but that is what gave the studying its structure: the five domains follow the order of the official objectives, in Portuguese, and none is treated superficially for being less technical. GRC, third-party risk, and incident response are exactly the topics the exam leans on and the ones most courses treat as an appendix.

The practice exams were the decisive factor. The questions follow the same format as the real exam: scenario wording, a capitalized keyword, two or three plausible options, and a single best answer. The course PBQs also come close to the ones that appear on exam day, which softens the impact of a first timed interactive simulation. I arrived at the Pearson VUE center with no surprises about the format, and the level of difficulty I had been encountering in the practice exams matched that of the exam itself.

For anyone studying in Portuguese and looking for a single route, from zero to booking the exam, this is the material I recommend.
